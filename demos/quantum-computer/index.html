The Quantum Computer

A machine that computes with the rules of atoms, built from microwave cables, gold-plated copper and one of the coldest places in the universe. Scroll to take the tour, from the lab down to a barrier a few atoms thick.

01 · The lab

The computer is the small part

The white cylinder is a refrigerator. The processor inside is the size of a postage stamp. Everything else in the room exists to keep it cold and to talk to it: racks of microwave electronics, helium pumps and a compressor.

02 · The fridge

Six plates, each colder than the last

Take the shields off and the chandelier appears. A dilution refrigerator cools in stages, from room temperature at the top to about a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero at the bottom. Outer space is 270 times warmer.

03 · Wiring

Every qubit has its own cables

Microwave pulses travel down coaxial lines to steer the qubits. At every plate, attenuators strip out the heat noise that rides along. The answers come back up so faint that they need an amplifier at 4 K before anything can read them. A few lines per qubit: this plumbing is a big part of why scaling is hard.

04 · Lights out

Switch off the daylight

…and you see what actually moves: microwave pulses going down, faint echoes coming back. From here on, everything that carries a signal glows.

05 · The chip

97 qubits on a postage stamp

Under the last shield sits the processor: a sliver of silicon with a pattern of aluminium on top. Each cross is one qubit. The lines around them carry pulses in and answers out.

06 · The qubit

An atom you can draw

A cross of aluminium, less than a millimetre across, cooled until it superconducts. Together with one tiny junction it behaves like a single atom with two energy levels. Call them 0 and 1. They sit about 5 GHz apart, which is why the controls are microwaves.

07 · The junction

A barrier a few atoms thick

Two layers of aluminium, separated by one to two nanometres of oxide. Pairs of electrons tunnel straight through it. This Josephson junction is the one component that turns an ordinary circuit into a qubit.

08 · Superposition

Not 0, not 1, a point on a sphere

A bit is a switch. A qubit is an arrow that can point anywhere on a sphere. The poles are 0 and 1, and everywhere else is a blend of both. Looking forces it to choose.

09 · Entanglement

Neighbours that share one state

Qubits talk only to their nearest neighbours, through couplers. A two-qubit gate entangles them: afterwards neither has a state of its own, only the pair does. Each extra qubit doubles what the chip holds in play at once.

10 · Error correction

Many fragile qubits, one reliable one

A qubit goes wrong about once in every few hundred operations. So one logical qubit is spread across many physical ones. Checks between them run nearly a million times a second, and a classical decoder undoes each error without ever looking at the data.

11 · Scale

One fridge is not enough

A useful machine needs hundreds of logical qubits, each built from hundreds of physical ones. That means many refrigerators wired into one computer, with classical supercomputers beside them doing the decoding.

12 · Purpose

Not faster. Different.

A quantum computer will not replace the gigafactory next door. It is a specialist: simulating molecules and materials the way nature computes them, and factoring the large numbers that today's encryption relies on. Luxembourg is getting its own. MeluXina-Q is planned to start with at least ten semiconductor spin qubits, a different kind of qubit from the one in this tour, wired into the MeluXina supercomputer in Bissen.
