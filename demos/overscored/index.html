What happens when a parent of a toddler gets access to too many tokens and has to listen to certain songs a couple thousand times.

Itsy Bitsy Spider · in the manner of Hans Zimmer

Waterspout

The tune at four speeds at once.

The tune is never varied. The basses play it one note per bar, so slowly that it becomes the harmony: every chord change is the next note of the song. The horns play it six times too slow. The strings fit the whole first line into every single bar.

The shape follows the story instead of the melody: a long climb, the rain that washes everything out, the sun, and then the climb again. Only when the sun comes out does a piano play the tune the way you know it.

Listen for

  • The climb: cellos alone, then one layer after another
  • The rain, with all of the brass
  • The sun. The dripping stops and the piano plays it straight
  • Again, until it is cut off
Forces
8 horns, 6 trombones, 2 tubas, organ, piano, synthesizer, wordless choir, strings and a dripping tap. No woodwinds; they were not asked.
Length
44 bars · about 2½ minutes

The Wheels on the Bus · in the manner of John Williams

Omnibus

The tune, taken apart into passengers.

The tune is cut into pieces and each piece goes to one section of the orchestra, which plays only its own bars: the wheels are bars 1 and 5, the horn is bars 2, 4 and 6, the doors are bar 3. The route is the tune’s own eight bars of harmony, driven five times.

One passenger boards per round, so the song starts full of holes and fills up stop by stop. The first violins wait 35 bars to play five notes. On the last round every seat is taken and the song is heard whole for the first time. The climb before it is a real omnibus progression, which is what harmony textbooks call that chord sequence.

Listen for

  • First run: wheels only, with holes where the rest of the song should be
  • The horn boards, from the next street
  • The doors
  • Up the hill
  • Terminus: the whole song, for the first time
Forces
Large orchestra with six horns. Empty staves are not printed, so the page fills up as the bus does.
Length
54 bars · just under 2 minutes

Au clair de la lune · in the manner of Erik Satie

Lunaison

The tune is not varied. It is lit.

One lunar month in nine phases. Every phase is the first line of the song over the same four chords; the only thing that changes is how much of the line is in the light. Notes in shadow are printed grey and are not played.

The moon fills from the right, so the tune appears from its last note backwards and is whole only at full moon. It empties from the right too, so the last thing left in the light is the three notes everybody knows. The left hand is the amount of light. Then a door closes.

Listen for

  • First crescent: only the last three notes
  • Full moon: the whole song, once
  • Last crescent: the three notes everybody knows
  • New moon, and the door
Forces
Piano, a pen (absent), a candle (dead) and a door, which has one note.
Length
48 bars · about 3½ minutes

Alle meine Entchen · in the manner of Richard Wagner

Der Ring des Entchens

The prelude: 136 bars over a single low E-flat.

Modelled on the opening of Das Rheingold: the same number of bars over the same bass note, rising from the bottom of the water. It works because this children’s song turns out to be a round. Sung by five voices two bars apart, it makes just two chords in alternation, so it can sit on one bass note for as long as anyone likes.

Under water you hear the tune upside down, entering at the bottom of the orchestra and climbing. From bar 77 each voice, as it finishes a line, starts the next one the right way up. At the surface eight horns enter two bars apart, like ducklings in a row. Then the curtain rises on the duck.

Listen for

  • The lake bed: double basses alone. Very low, so use headphones
  • Under water: the tune upside down
  • Surfacing, one voice at a time
  • The surface: eight horns in a row
  • The brass, at half speed
  • The duck
Forces
A Ring-sized orchestra with eight horns and six harps, and one duck.
Length
136 bars · about 4 minutes

Schlaf, Kindlein, schlaf · in the manner of Gustav Mahler

Dritte Nachtmusik

Everything falls.

Five verses, each one line shorter than the last and exactly as much slower, so every verse lasts the same 33 seconds. Each is a third lower, one dynamic softer and sung by a lower instrument: violins, English horn, a horn far away, one cello, one double bass.

The last verse stops before its final word. The note it would have landed on is the end of the tune and the next step down the chain of thirds, and it is never played. Then come 33 more seconds with no lines at all. The herd bells are the father with the sheep, the mandolin is the mother shaking the tree, and the celesta is the dream falling out of it.

Listen for

  • First verse: all five lines, in the violins
  • Second verse: a canon that nobody finishes
  • Third verse: a horn from far away, with the herd bells
  • Fourth verse: one cello, barely awake
  • Fifth verse: one double bass, one line, one note short
  • No lines at all
Forces
A Mahler-sized orchestra with herd bells, mandolin and celesta. The four trumpets are asleep before it starts, and the page empties as the players drop off.
Length
34 bars · about 3½ minutes

How these were made

I picked the tunes and the composers and kept asking for more. Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, wrote the music, engraved the scores with LilyPond and rendered the recordings with FluidSynth. The recordings are synthesised, so the orchestra is keener than it is refined.
